How can two Besu databases holding the same state differ 8× in performance?

A case study on state-actor versus a Bonsai mainnet-snapshot database, and on why a published snapshot has to be compacted before it can be measured at all.

The behaviour

Three databases hold the same state. The same 1,100 EEST tests run against each of them, on the same host and the same NVMe drive. 660 of those tests do account-state work; the other 440 are controls that run the same loop and deliberately touch no state, and they are kept out of every comparison below and reported on their own. Gas matches across all three to six significant figures, so each one was asked to do the same work. Throughput does not match. On the tests that look up an account which is not there, the generated store is 8.1× slower than the published snapshot.

That is the number in the title. It is also mostly an accident of how the two stores were built, rather than anything about how Besu reads them. There are two separate accidents, and each one shows up on a different kind of read.

Every dot below is one test category against the compacted snapshot, and they fall into two groups about ten times apart: the 8 that must prove an account is absent, and the 40 that read one which exists. Two groups, two causes.

Outside the absent class, the residual comes in two tiers

Against the compacted snapshot, every one of the 40 categories that read an account which exists favours the snapshot, by a median of 5.9%. They do not all favour it equally, and the split is not noise: the 24 categories whose contract code is shared or absent sit at 5.2%, while the 16 that read a distinct contract on every access sit at 17.7%. That gap is the subject of the last section. Everything larger than it is a property of how one of the stores was built.

What Besu's own logs and counters say

Besu states its own configuration at startup, and two of those lines matter.

Existing database at /data. Metadata versionedStorageFormat=BaseVersionedStorageFormat{format=BONSAI, version=3}. Processing WAL...

That one appears on every boot of every arm, including the arm whose write-ahead log is 23 bytes long, so it is boot-time boilerplate rather than evidence of work. The second is the one that shapes the rest:

Flat db mode found FULL

Account reads go to Bonsai's flat keyspace, not down the trie. The metrics agree 99.83% of account lookups are served from the flat database.

That matters because it fixes what a read costs. An account lookup is one key in one column family, so the cost is the cost of locating that key: how many files have to be consulted, and how many bytes each consultation moves. It is not a tree walk whose depth grows with the state, which is the shape most people expect.

Besu counts flat-database lookups that find no key. The absence categories probe addresses that are genuinely absent from both stores, so the difference in what they cost is not a difference in what they find.

What we ruled out first

Both stores run byte-identical RocksDB settings on every state column family, compression=kLZ4Compression and block_size=32,768. The extraction is deterministic across three independent unpacks. Trie logs are disabled on both. Whole-store totals are not comparable, because the snapshot carries 760 GB of chain history the generated store does not have, so every comparison in this article is per column family.

What the fixtures actually touch

The suite is 48 opcode/account-mode categories across 11 gas budgets from 100M to 300M. They split into three groups that behave so differently that any single median over them describes none of them:

  • absent (110 tests): NON_EXISTING_ACCOUNT targets, where the lookup must prove the account is not there.
  • leaf-only (198 tests): BALANCE, or an EXISTING_EOA target, which reads the account record and never the code.
  • code-reading (792 tests): EXTCODE* and the CALL family into a contract, which also reads the code blob.

The file that looks like the cause: a 1.2 GB write-ahead log

The published snapshot ships a write-ahead log of 1,258,464,804 bytes, which is exactly the shape of a benchmark artifact: state that lives in one place on disk and another in memory, restored on every boot.

It is inert. Its size varies 9% between identical replays, a poor cause for an effect that reproduces. Draining it adds no SST file, because RocksDB's open path had already recovered and flushed it before any test ran. And measured end to end, draining changes nothing.

The root cause: the pre-run's rows are the newest versions of their keys

Before each test the harness replays a pre-run bundle: 10.06 GB of blocks that create the accounts the benchmark then reads. It takes the store from 6,695 SST files to 6,699. Four files is not much of a change; what matters is that every account the benchmark reads now has its newest version in one of them.

The compaction proves it, by what it deletes. Merging the levels of the account column family removes 10,833,266 entries, 365,626,139 down to 354,792,873, and a merge can only drop an entry if it was an obsolete older version of a key held somewhere else in the tree. The same thing happens in storage and trie-branch:

A store holding only one version of each key cannot lose entries to a compaction. These do, so the pre-run's writes were sitting above older copies of the same keys.

A point lookup in a levelled store checks the youngest files first and stops at the first version it finds. After the pre-run the account column family holds 4 files at the top of the tree, 18 MB of it, above 273 files holding 16.7 GB at the bottom. The reads the benchmark performs are satisfied in the small, young part of the tree.

Compaction merges all of it into the bottom level: 0 files left at the top, and the same lookup now reads a 32,768-byte block out of a sorted run of 17.9 GB with no locality to whatever was written beside it. The cost of a read goes up while the store gets smaller, which is the signature of removing an accident rather than applying an optimisation.

The fix, and the proof

A full-range compaction takes 91 minutes and leaves the store smaller: 6,699 files and 382.7 GB become 5,454 and 360.3 GB. Most of the time goes to the largest column families: cf09 3104s, cf08 1477s, cf01 414s.

Throughput and bytes read, each against the plain snapshot. The drain contributes nothing; the compaction contributes everything. Neither moves the control, so neither is doing something to the harness rather than to the store.

Now measure the generated store against the treated snapshot instead of the published one. Nothing about the generated store changed, so whatever moves is the artifact leaving. Take agreement to within ±10% of parity as the bar: 0 of 48 categories cleared it against the snapshot as published, and 24 of 48 clear it against the compacted one. Per measurement workload rather than per category, 0% becomes 52%.

Clearing a ±10% band is not agreeing. Not one of the 48 categories reaches parity, the closest being CALLCODE EOA at 0.960×, and not one of the 660 measurement workloads is faster than the snapshot.

The 24 that converge are the categories whose contract code is shared or absent, and they land at a median of 5.2% off parity, down from 83.8%. Two groups stay outside, with two different causes already named: the 8 absence categories, still 9.1× apart, and the 16 that read a distinct contract on every access, which close to 17.7% and no further. The first is the next section; the second is the last one.

Why absence proofs cost 50× the bytes: the generated store has no bloom filters

The absence categories probe addresses that are missing from both stores. The counters in the second section put the miss rate at 0.955 and 0.999. Identical question, identical answer, and 50.1× the bytes to arrive at it.

The reason is in the files. Every state column family in the snapshot carries a bloom filter; the generated store's carry none at all.

column familyfilter bytes, snapshotfilter bytes, state-actorpolicy, state-actor
cf06 ACCOUNT_INFO_STATE443,500,6730(none)

cf08 ACCOUNT_STORAGE_STORAGE2,278,300,3550(none)
cf09 TRIE_BRANCH_STORAGE3,766,897,4590(none)
A bloom filter answers “this file cannot contain that key” without reading the file. Without one, the lookup reads index and data blocks to reach the same conclusion.

The cost lands almost entirely on absence. A lookup that finds its key was going to read that block anyway, so the filters buy little there, 1.21× on leaf-only and 1.32× on code-reading, against 50.1× when there is nothing to find. This is a property of how the generated store was written, not of what it contains.

This one was already fixed upstream before the store was built. state-actor #133 (11389bc) put a full bloom filter at 10 bits/key on every column family and merged on 2026-08-04; the store measured here was generated on 2026-09-09 from a working tree (e4cb205-dirty) that predated it by 36 days. So the 50× is real, reproducible from the archived store, and our own doing: we measured a build that had already been superseded.

The residual

What is left after both artifacts is small, consistent, and in two tiers. Against the compacted snapshot every one of the 40 categories that reads an existing account favours the snapshot, but the 24 whose code is shared or absent sit at 5.2% while the 16 that read a distinct contract per access sit at 17.7%. Order the classes by how much unique contract code they touch and you have ordered the residual, which is the whole of this section.

The chain: generated records are larger and compress worse, so a data block holds fewer of them and every read moves more bytes.

Compression 1.182× and block size 1.180×; geth measured 1.167× and 1.166× on a different engine, so this is not an artifact of one. The cause is a single property: mainnet reuses each bytecode about 28 times, and the generated store reused none, 68,120,232 contract accounts resolving to 2,416,222 distinct bytecodes against 135,238,776 resolving to 134,442,676. A shared code hash compresses and a unique one does not, and a code read pays block padding for its neighbours. #137 and #138 fix both: designators from a fixed pool of 256, bytecode from a shared pool sized to mainnet's reuse. On a store built from them, account records go from 0.513 to 0.447 physical over logical against the snapshot's 0.434.

The code half is not the code being read

The same chain does not explain the 16 distinct-code categories. Subtracting the account-only classes isolates the code read: 24.1 extra bytes per gas on the snapshot against 47.3 on the generated store, a factor of 1.97. The obvious reading, less compressible code, is wrong: the 24,576-byte fixture contracts deflate to 0.0109 and 0.0070 of their size, near-free to store in both.

What a lookup pays for is the block. RocksDB closes a data block past 32,768 bytes, so a 24,576-byte contract shares it with whatever follows in code-hash order, a sample of each store's contract population.

Modelled from each store's own code column family by packing records the way RocksDB does. The snapshot's co-tenants are a couple of mainnet contracts that compress; the generated store's are 32 small ones that do not. That is 1.81× on the block against 1.97× measured on the read.

The generated store held 134,442,676 contracts averaging 398 bytes against the snapshot's 2,416,222 averaging 7,671: many times as many, each a fraction of the size, every one unique. A code read pays for its neighbours. That is fixable by shrinking the block or by giving the generator a mainnet-shaped population, and the second was taken.

#138 went too far. Its pool is one real ERC20 runtime of about 1.7 KB, tiled to the sampled size and rotated per entry, so entries are self-similar inside themselves and to each other. On a store built from it the code column family compresses to 0.056 physical over logical against mainnet's 0.371, 6.6× too compressible. Mainnet code compresses because many different contracts repeat across accounts, not because one repeats inside itself. The fix is a corpus.

Measured rather than modelled: one cold read of a 24,576-byte contract, page cache dropped, moves 17,597 bytes from disk on the generated store against 901 on the snapshot, a factor of 20, for the same contracts in both.

Where this stands

All three fixes are merged, and the suite has been run again against a store regenerated from them. The expectations in the table below were written down before that store existed, from store-level measurements alone; the last column is what the rerun measured. 660 measurement workloads and 440 controls, the same grid as the rest of this article, with gas identical to six figures on every one of the 1,100 tests.

Two of the three went where the store-level numbers said they would. The absence class, 8.5× apart and the reason this article has an 8× in its title, is at 1.002×: gone, not reduced. The classes whose code is shared or absent moved from 0.947 to 0.985×, which is close to parity and not at it.

The third did not close. It inverted: the categories that read a distinct contract on every access went from 0.828× to 1.461×, so the generated store is now 46.1% faster than the snapshot on exactly the reads it used to be slower on. That is the 6.6× over-compression of the previous section arriving as throughput. A store that is too fast is as wrong as a store that is too slow, and it is harder to notice, because nothing looks broken.

The gas budget separates the two outcomes cleanly. Across 11 budgets from 100M to 300M the absence class now sits flat between 0.984 and 1.028 with no trend, where before it fell from 0.147 to 0.099. A flat line is what a removed mechanism looks like; a shallower slope would only be a smaller one. The distinct-code class does the opposite and climbs monotonically from 1.364 to 1.512: the more reads a block does, the further ahead the over-compressed store gets.

One caveat. A stateful fixture is anchored to the genesis of the store it was filled against, so the rerun needed refilled payloads and the two arms cannot share a bundle. The control workloads, which touch no account state, say the comparison survived that: 1.0164× against 1.0187× on the original arm, flat within 0.009 across every budget.

The corpus fix, measured

#141 replaces the tiled runtime with windows into 128 real mainnet contracts, one contract per pool entry, never tiled. On the same 4 GB basis as the 0.056 above, a store built from it compresses its code column family to 0.495 physical over logical, its records deflate to 0.438 against mainnet's 0.443, and the co-tenant payload beside a fixture contract to 0.416 against 0.439 measured on the snapshot with the same probe: the pool now compresses like the population it stands in for. The account column family, which the pool does not touch, reads 0.429.

Only the sixteen distinct-code categories were refilled and rerun, against a 350 GB store generated the same way as the previous rerun's, at 3 gas budgets: 64 measurement workloads and 60 controls, gas identical to six figures on every one. The other classes never read the pool, and a spot check of 2 absent and 2 shared-code workloads at 100M put them at 0.968 and 0.970×, where the previous rerun had 1.018 and 0.979.

The distinct-code class is at 1.309×: 0.828 before the fixes, 1.461 under the tiled pool, still 30.9% faster than the snapshot and outside the ±10% band. 16 of 16 categories moved toward parity, the rows spanning 1.031 to 1.393×, and the budget gradient still climbs, 1.214 to 1.367.

What is left is not compressibility, and it is not the pool. Per workload the generated store moves 0.77 to 0.73 of what the snapshot moves for the same gas, where the original store moved 1.43 to 1.49, and throughput follows that byte ratio at every budget. Those bytes are not the opcode's own lookups: a 100M test counts 1,306 account reads and moves 7.4 GB. Besu prefetches every account in the block access list, and the two arms prefetch the same ones, 31,514 against 31,889 at 100M and within 1.2% at every budget. Same work, different cost per lookup.

KiB read from disk per prefetched account, the workload's dominant cost. The code record is 2.4 KiB of that, which is why the pool cannot be the term. An account lookup costs 22 KiB on the snapshot volume against 17 on the generated one, and a trie node 31 against 22, at the same bytes per data block: the snapshot answers one lookup with 1.28 block reads, the generated store with exactly 1.00.

That is layout, and it puts a floor under the comparison. The same snapshot, same host, same harness, same gas, costs 41 KiB per prefetched account as it ships and 225 KiB after nothing but a full compaction, a swing of 6 to 14 times across the budgets, and only on the classes that read contract code. A store written once in hash order has no recently written region to cluster in, so it is in the scattered regime by construction, and the two scattered stores sit within 25% of each other. The corpus pool moved that from 32%. The remainder is smaller than the reference's own sensitivity to a housekeeping decision, which is where this measurement ends.

The other 8 budgets, run afterwards as a second arm of 320 tests, fill the gradient in monotonically, 1.235 at 120M to 1.357 at 280M, and the byte ratio with it, 0.765 to 0.733. That arm's controls sat at 0.989× against the archived 1.019, outside the tolerance this comparison is held to, so its points are shown and not counted.

The control canary on this arm: 1.0120× against 1.019× on the original, per-budget medians within 0.001.

What this article does not settle
  • Why the code-reusing classes stop at 1.5% off parity rather than reaching it. The cf06 block geometry is the right shape, 1.18× predicted against 1.21× measured, but the remainder has no mechanism named.
  • Why an account lookup takes 1.28 data-block reads on the snapshot volume and exactly 1.00 on a generated one. That asymmetry is the whole of the remaining 30.9%, and it is measured here, not explained.
  • Why the control sits at 1.9% and 1.6% rather than at parity on either arm. Close enough to keep the comparison, never attributed.
  • The snapshot ships 246 cache files totalling 6.3 GB, never examined here.
  • Whether the pre-run's placement advantage is purely level position or partly block cache residency; compaction changes both at once.
